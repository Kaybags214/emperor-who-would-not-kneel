Chapter 5: The Heavenly Envoy

The Wastes had no morning.

There was only darkness thinning by degrees.

A pale gray light spread across the broken plain, revealing what night had hidden: black stone, collapsed towers, rusted weapons half-buried in ash, and the remains of roads that led nowhere. In the distance, a dead city leaned against the horizon like a wounded animal refusing to fall.

Egris stood at the edge of the camp and watched the souls behind him wake.

They did not wake as the living did.

There was no stretching, no yawning, no complaints about cold ground or aching limbs. The dead rose quietly. Some stared at their hands as if they had only just remembered they possessed them. Others searched the faces around them, still hoping to find someone they had lost.

A woman in a torn red dress sat beside a low fire that gave no warmth. She held the hand of a boy who looked no older than ten. The boy had been among the souls Egris had pulled from the Hollow Men two nights before.

He had not spoken since.

Near the ruined wall of an old watchtower, three soldiers argued in whispers over a blade with no edge. One wore the armor of a kingdom Egris did not recognize. Another wore something closer to the dress of a desert warrior. The third had been dead so long that his clothes had become little more than strips of gray cloth hanging from his frame.

They had been enemies once.

Egris could see it in the way they watched one another.

Even here, beyond life, men carried their wars with them.

He understood that.

He had carried his own.

"You have been standing there for too long."

Egris did not turn. "Then you have been watching me for too long."

The old soldier behind him gave a dry laugh.

He was called Borchu, though Egris did not know whether that had been his real name. Few souls in the Wastes remembered their lives clearly enough to offer such things with certainty. Borchu had been a soldier before death, that much was obvious. He stood straight despite the wound that had split the left side of his face. No blood came from it. Nothing came from any wound here. But the mark remained.

A memory of how he had died.

"You watch them as if they are already yours," Borchu said.

"They are not mine."

"No?"

Egris finally looked at him.

The old soldier's expression did not change.

"They followed you," Borchu continued. "They sleep near your fire. They wait for you to speak before they move. If that is not ownership, what is it?"

"It is caution."

"Caution is what prey feels when a wolf enters the valley."

Egris's eyes narrowed.

Borchu did not bow his head. That was why Egris tolerated him.

"Then perhaps they should stop behaving like prey," Egris said.

The old soldier nodded once, as though Egris had confirmed something for him.

Before either man could speak again, the air changed.

The fire behind them went out.

Not dimmed.

Not flickered.

It simply ceased to exist.

Every soul in the camp went still.

The gray light across the Wastes withdrew as if something had swallowed it. The ruins darkened. The ashes beneath Egris's boots began to rise in thin spirals, turning slowly around him.

Then the sky opened.

There was no sound at first.

Only a line of white fire appearing above the broken plain.

It cut through the darkness from one horizon to the other.

The dead fell to their knees.

Some did so willingly.

Others looked confused, horrified by their own bodies as their legs folded beneath them. The boy in the camp cried out and clung to the woman beside him. The three soldiers near the ruined tower pressed their faces to the ground.

Borchu remained standing.

Egris did as well.

The white line widened.

A shape descended through it.

At first it seemed to be a man wrapped in light. Then Egris saw the armor: bright metal without scratches, fitted over a tall frame like a second skin. A long cloak of white feathers moved behind him, though there was no wind. He carried no sword in his hands, but one hung at his side, its blade burning within its sheath.

His face was almost human.

Almost.

It was too still. Too perfect. Its eyes held no reflection of the ruined land below.

The figure touched the ground several paces from Egris.

Where his feet met the ash, flowers rose.

Small white things, fragile and impossible.

The dead watched them in silence.

The envoy's gaze moved over the camp, lingering on those still kneeling. Then it came to rest on Egris.

"Egris."

His name carried across the Wastes with the force of a command.

Egris did not move.

"You know me," he said.

"I know what you were."

"No," Egris replied. "You know what you were told."

For the first time, something changed in the envoy's face.

Not anger.

Not surprise.

Only the faintest shadow of recognition.

"I am Seraphel," the figure said. "General of the Seventh Host. Voice of the Throne Beyond the Gate."

"A long name."

"A necessary one."

"Nothing is necessary except death."

At that, a murmur passed through the camp.

The kneeling souls glanced toward one another. Some looked toward the sky. Others stared at Egris as if waiting to see whether lightning would strike him where he stood.

Seraphel's eyes turned briefly toward the ruins beyond the camp.

"The Wastes have made you proud."

"The Wastes have made me awake."

"You were offered a place."

"I was offered a chain."

The angel's expression hardened by a fraction.

"God offered you purpose."

"God offered me obedience."

"Order."

"Obedience wearing a better mask."

The white flowers beneath Seraphel's feet grew taller.

Egris watched them curl around the envoy's boots.

"You speak of chains," Seraphel said, "yet already the dead gather behind you. Already they watch you as soldiers watch a commander. You call Heaven a prison because you fear being ruled. But what will you call the empire you intend to build?"

Egris looked back at the camp.

At the frightened child.

At the woman holding him.

At the soldiers who had not yet learned to stand beside former enemies without reaching for weapons.

At Borchu, who stood with his arms folded, waiting.

"I have not built an empire."

"No. Not yet."

Seraphel stepped closer.

The air around him was warm now. It smelled of rain, clean stone, and something Egris remembered from a life that seemed farther away with every passing moment: open grass beneath a blue sky.

"Heaven does not seek to destroy you," the envoy said. "The Throne sees your strength. It sees your will. It knows what you could become here."

"Then it should be afraid."

"It is not."

"Everything should fear what it cannot control."

For a moment, Seraphel said nothing.

Then he raised one hand.

The darkness around them vanished.

The camp disappeared.

The Wastes disappeared.

Egris stood on a high hill beneath a vast golden sky.

Below him lay a city greater than any he had ever seen.

Its towers were white and gold. Rivers crossed it in shining lines. Roads ran outward in perfect order, joining distant kingdoms beneath one banner. There were no ruined homes. No burning fields. No starving children hiding beneath carts. Men and women moved through wide streets without fear.

There were soldiers, too.

Thousands of them.

But they carried their weapons lowered.

No one screamed.

No one ran.

No one bowed in terror.

Egris felt the weight of the vision settle around him.

"A world at peace," Seraphel said beside him.

Egris looked down at the city.

"Whose peace?"

"The peace of the redeemed."

"Those who obey."

"Those who understand."

He looked toward Seraphel.

"And those who do not?"

The city below remained beautiful.

Too beautiful.

Far in the distance, beyond its gates, Egris saw a long road winding toward a wall of white stone. Along that road walked a line of souls, quiet and orderly, each wearing the same pale garments. None looked left or right.

None spoke.

"They are given their place," Seraphel said.

"They are assigned it."

"They are spared confusion."

"They are spared choice."

The vision trembled.

The golden sky broke apart.

The hill vanished, and Egris stood once more in the Wastes.

The flowers were gone.

The fire in the camp remained dead.

Seraphel looked less like light now and more like a weapon forged to resemble a man.

"You mistake freedom for strength," the angel said. "You mistake defiance for wisdom. God does not demand your submission because He needs another servant. He demands it because every soul requires a place in the order of things."

Egris stepped forward until only a few feet separated them.

"I had a place once."

Seraphel's gaze did not leave his.

"I was born weak enough for men to believe they could break me. I watched allies turn against one another. I watched men swear loyalty in the morning and betray it before nightfall. I watched rulers call themselves chosen while their people starved beneath them."

His voice remained quiet.

That made the camp listen more closely.

"I did not survive because some higher power gave me a place. I survived because I took one."

The angel's eyes flickered.

"You took it with blood."

"Yes."

"With fear."

"Yes."

"With conquest."

Egris's jaw tightened.

"Yes."

The word hung between them.

It was not a defense.

It was not an apology.

Seraphel studied him as though seeing the shape of his soul for the first time.

Then the angel reached for the sword at his side.

The dead recoiled.

Borchu moved toward Egris, but Egris lifted one hand without looking back.

Stay.

Seraphel drew the blade.

Its light was so bright that the shadows of every soul in the camp stretched across the ground behind them.

"I have been commanded to bring you before the Throne," Seraphel said.

"Then you should have brought an army."

"I did."

The sky opened again.

This time, it did not reveal a single line of white fire.

It revealed hundreds.

They descended like falling stars.

Shapes moved within them—winged figures in armor, carrying spears of light and banners that bore no symbol Egris could see. The ground shook as they approached. The Hollow Men had hunted the Wastes like animals.

These beings came like a judgment.

Fear spread through the camp.

The woman grabbed the boy and pulled him close. The soldiers stumbled backward. Some of the kneeling souls began to pray.

Egris heard the prayers.

Not because they were loud.

Because they were desperate.

Seraphel pointed his sword toward Egris.

"Kneel," he said. "Accept the mercy offered to you. Let these souls enter the light. Let order be restored."

Egris looked at the dead behind him.

They were afraid.

They had every reason to be.

Then he looked upward at the descending host.

He had stood before armies before. He had watched men tremble while waiting for his command. He had known what it meant to send others toward death while remaining alive long enough to count the cost.

But these were not his soldiers.

Not yet.

He could not command their loyalty.

He could not demand their courage.

So he turned to them.

"Stand," Egris said.

No one moved.

The white light continued to fall.

Egris raised his voice.

"I said stand."

The boy was the first.

His legs shook as he pushed himself upright. The woman rose beside him, still holding his hand. Borchu stood with them. Then one soldier rose. Then another.

Slowly, unevenly, the camp stood.

Not because they believed they could win.

Not because Egris had promised them safety.

But because they had been given a choice.

Seraphel's face became cold.

"You would lead them into destruction."

"No," Egris said. "I would let them decide whether they live on their knees."

The angel lifted his blade.

Egris reached down and pulled an old spear from the ash.

The wood was cracked. The metal head was dark with age.

It was not enough.

But it was in his hand.

Above them, Heaven's army descended.

And for the first time since entering the Wastes, Egris did not stand alone.
